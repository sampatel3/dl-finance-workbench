Verdict: no

I know what is being asked of me. I would not say yes on this deck alone.

The ask itself is the strongest part of the deck: one bad month-end, an hour, whoever owns the
figure, and a written verdict including "don't do this". That is clear, cheap and well
constructed. What I cannot do is justify the hour to anybody else in my building, because the
deck never states a single thing I get out of the product. It tells me, at length and rather
well, how a figure is traced. It never tells me what that is worth, that it has ever been done
on real ledgers, or which part of my month it removes. An hour is cheap, but the hour leads to
US$185K, and the deck gives me nothing to put in the sentence "we should look at this because
—".

## Blocking

- **High** — There is no value claim anywhere in the deck. Not a quantified one, not even a
  directional one. The problem slide says "Twelve times a year, the same work" and no later
  slide claims that work gets smaller, faster, cheaper or safer. The verbs on the product
  slides are "opens", "names", "drills", "reaches" — all about mechanism, none about outcome.
  I would have to see, on the face of the deck, what changes for the finance function: days
  off the close, questions answered without a rebuild, people not doing the rebuild, arguments
  that stop. Any one of them, stated as a claim I could hold you to.
  - The only outcome numbers in the whole deck (72h→15min, 67%→95%, 2,000+ users) belong to a
    different product for a different client in a different industry. They are on the
    credentials slide, not the product slides, and a careful reader notices that the product
    itself is described entirely without numbers of its own.

- **High** — Nothing in the deck says the workbench has ever run on real finance data. Every
  figure on slides 4, 5 and 6 comes from "a synthetic group" — which the deck honourably
  discloses — and the twelve-week plan then puts "back-tested against closes your controllers
  already stood behind" at weeks 4–8. Read plainly, that says the first real close this thing
  is measured against is mine. There is no reference customer for this product, no pilot, no
  "we did this at X and it reproduced eleven of twelve months". I would have to see either one
  named engagement where the workbench ran on a real ledger and what it found, or an explicit
  sentence owning that I would be the first and saying what protects me if week 8 fails.

- **High** — The deck's flagship promise is never shown. The title tagline is "Every figure in
  your close, opened to the rows that made it"; slide 6's headline is "Each figure drills to
  the rows that made it". The screenshot on slide 6 stops at the five entities, and the panel
  that would hold the rows — "Technical evidence: Formula, provenance and source rows" — is
  shut, which the deck's own alt text concedes ("closed until it is opened"). Not one ledger
  row appears anywhere in the deck. The single capability the whole argument rests on is the
  one thing the reader is asked to take on trust. I would have to see the evidence panel open,
  with real (synthetic is fine) rows, accounts and vintages visible on the slide.

- **High** — The deck contradicts itself about what the product actually takes off my desk.
  The "Why Now" slide puts variance, forecast, scenario and "the commentary a board reads" in
  the **Decision** layer, tagged **"Where the work is"** and glossed "still a person, every
  month" — and puts the workbench in the layer *below* it, tagged "Missing". The standfirst on
  that same slide says the gap is to "connect the pieces, **interpret the movement and explain
  it to a board**". So: does the workbench write the commentary or not? Its own diagram says
  no; its own prose says yes; and the screenshot on the very next slide contains written
  interpretation ("Because of Kestrel Manufacturing Ltd, up £285k — 46.1% of the movement,
  owned by UK Financial Controller"). Commentary is the most expensive thing in my month, and
  after reading the deck twice I still cannot tell you whether you claim to produce it. I would
  have to see one consistent statement of what the layer does and what stays human, with the
  diagram and the prose agreeing.

- **High** — The commitment behind the ask is disclosed only as a caption on a slide about
  something else. "From US$185K · one workflow, live" sits in the middle card of the engagement
  slide; the ask slide names no money at all. And US$185K is only the build. Nothing tells me
  what the workbench costs to keep: licence or subscription, per user or per entity, who hosts
  it, what year two looks like, or what "from" is a function of. I am being asked to start a
  path whose total cost I cannot bound. I would have to see, at minimum, the twelve-week price
  and the run cost after it, on the same page as the ask.

- **High** — The deck asks for five ledgers of transaction-level data and never says where that
  data goes. No deployment model (my tenant, your cloud, on-premise), no data residency, no
  statement of who at Deeplight can see GL rows, no mention of what happens to my data if we
  stop at week 3. The group depicted spans the UK, Germany, the US and the Gulf, and your own
  credentials are regulated in the UAE, so this is the first question my CIO asks and the
  second my auditor asks. "The controls your audit function asks for" is named as a week-9
  deliverable, which is after my data has been in your hands for eight weeks. I would have to
  see one line on where it runs and one on who can see it.

- **High** — "Why Now" does not answer why now. The headline under it — "Keep the accounting
  system. The gap is the layer above it." — answers *where this fits*, which is a different and
  already-made point. Nothing in the deck dates anything: no change in technology, cost,
  regulation or expectation that makes this possible in 2026 and not 2022. The only "now" is
  the word itself, in "the work a governed layer on top can **now** take on", which asserts the
  claim rather than supporting it. I would have to see what changed. Absent that, the honest
  answer to "why not next year" is "no reason given".

- **High** — Three of twelve surfaces are shown and the other nine are never named. The title
  slide sells "three of the twelve surfaces running live"; the contents page promises
  "Roadmap"; the slide that arrives under that name is the twelve-week delivery plan. So the
  deck claims a product four times larger than what it shows, and then never tells me what the
  other three quarters are or when I would get them. Either name the twelve, or drop the number
  and claim only what you show.

- **Low** — The deck's proudest number collides with the adjacent screenshot. Slide 5 leads
  with "**£0k** unattributed", while the screenshot on slide 4 carries a line reading
  "Eliminations and unattributed +£17k 2.8%" and a note that "The other 2 are in the
  remainder". Both are defensible in their own frames — bridge residual versus entity remainder
  — but the deck uses one word for two things on facing pages, and the reader who notices
  concludes the £0k is a definitional choice rather than an achievement.

- **Low** — The structure the contents page promises is not the structure that arrives. The
  contents lists four numbered sections (01 Problem, 02 Solution, 03 Company, 04 The Ask) and
  five sub-items; both dividers in the deck are numbered **03**, so "The Solution" arrives
  labelled 03 when the contents calls it 02. Two promised sub-items, "Our Team" and
  "Engagement", never appear as a section name — the team is folded into "The Company" and the
  engagement slide's eyebrow reads "How We Work", a name the contents page does not contain. A
  reader navigating by section name cannot find two of the nine things they were promised.

- **Low** — Slides 4 and 6 make the same claim twice. "Every figure on this screen opens into
  the working behind it" and "Each figure drills to the rows that made it" are one proposition,
  and with the tagline that is three statements of traceability out of eleven slides. One of
  those two slots would be better spent on the value claim or the proof that is missing above.

- **Low** — A grammatical error is baked into a screenshot: "not every posting carries a
  entity", in the note under the revenue-by-entity table on slide 4. `deck lint` cannot see
  inside a JPEG, so I am flagging it here.

## Would improve

- The cash line in the slide 4 screenshot is down 18.4% against forecast, which is the most
  alarming number in the deck, and no prose on any slide acknowledges it. A reader's eye goes
  there. Either use it — that is exactly the figure a board argues about — or the slide is
  showing a crisis it declines to mention.
- "Governed" carries a great deal of weight (governed reporting layer, governed model, governed
  layer) and is never defined in terms an auditor would recognise: approvals, segregation of
  duties, who signs, what is locked after sign-off. The week-9 and week-12 language gestures at
  it; the product slides do not.
- The team slide names four people and closes with "The same four from the first conversation
  to a live close. Nothing handed over a wall." A twelve-week build with ERP connections,
  permissions and pipeline monitoring is not delivered by a CEO, a CTO, a CPO and a CCO. Either
  the four are the accountable spine and the builders exist behind them — say so — or the claim
  invites the reader to doubt the plan. Nothing tells me how big Deeplight is, which matters
  when I am asked to run a close on their software.
- The engagement slide says "we say which one fits, including when none does", and the ask slide
  repeats it. It is a good line, said twice, thirty words apart on consecutive slides.
- Slide 5's standfirst says "Currency is separated out so no commercial bar carries a
  translation effect", and there is no currency bar in the bridge for the reader to check that
  against. A separated FX effect, shown as a number, would turn an assertion into evidence.

## What it gets right

- The headlines are claims, not labels. Read alone and in order they make an argument a skimming
  reader can actually follow: the ledger is done and the insight is manual → keep your ERP, the
  gap is above it → figures open into their working → a variance names its cause → twelve weeks
  to a real close → the people who scope it build it → bring us one problem. Almost no deck
  passes the headline-only test and this one does.
- The internal arithmetic holds up to checking. £12.4m less £11.8m is the £618k on slide 6; the
  bridge bars (582 + 22 − 19 + 83 − 50) sum to it; the five entities less the £855k elimination
  reconcile to £12.4m; five entities across GBP, EUR, USD and AED are the "four currencies" of
  slide 2. I tried to catch it out and could not.
- It volunteers its own conditions, which is rare and buys real credit: the demo group is
  synthetic and says so, "it stops at the ledger boundary: what the workbench cannot see, it
  says so", "for this to hold on your own data, the comparator has to be a versioned artefact
  rather than a spreadsheet somebody keeps", the plan is "stoppable at every step", and I keep
  the baseline whatever happens. The ask includes the possibility of being told no.

## Questions it failed to pre-empt

- What do I get? Days, hours, headcount, errors, arguments — any unit. The deck never says.
- Has this ever run on a real general ledger, at anyone? If not, am I the first?
- Does it write the board commentary, or not? The diagram and the prose disagree.
- Where does my ledger data live, who can see it, and what happens to it if I stop at week 3?
- What does it cost to keep after the twelve weeks — licence, hosting, support, per what?
- What are the other nine surfaces, and when would I have them?
- Why now rather than next year?
- Which of my systems can it actually read? "ERP and consolidation connections" is a week-9
  line item, not a list. I do not know whether my ERP is on it.
- Who does the work, beyond the four named? And how big is Deeplight?
- What happens at week 8 if the back-test does not reproduce a close my controllers signed —
  who eats that, and what do I have to show for eleven weeks?
- What could a board actually see? Nothing in the deck shows an output that leaves the tool —
  a pack, a page, an export. My month ends in a document, and the deck never shows one.

## What I made of the product after driving it

Nothing, deliberately. My brief made me the one reviewer on this round forbidden the running
product: I did not open http://localhost:3002, did not drive any surface, and did not read the
application source to settle anything the deck left open. Every gap above is a gap in the deck
as an artefact that has to survive being forwarded and skimmed with no demo attached, which is
how it will mostly be read.

What I did examine is the deck itself and its own assets: `web/public/deck.html`, the frame and
fallback behaviour in `web/public/deck.js` and `web/public/deck.css`, and the three stills the
deck actually ships on its product slides —
`web/public/shots/july-position.jpg`, `revenue-bridge.jpg` and `source-drill.jpg`. Two things
worth recording from that.

First, the forwarding case is sound: the live embed degrades to the captured still whenever the
app is not reachable or the file is opened from disk, so a reader in an inbox sees the
screenshots rather than three empty frames. That is the right default and it is documented.

Second, `web/public/shots/` contains ten captured stills, of which the deck uses three. The
seven it does not use are named `published-commentary`, `controls-checks`, `forecast-quality`,
`scenario-impact`, `version-diff`, `permission-refusal` and `cash-horizon` — which reads like
the list of answers to most of the questions I finished the deck still holding. I am not
judging the product on filenames, and I have not opened those images to see whether they hold
what their names suggest. But it does sharpen the finding: the deck spends three of its eleven
slides proving traceability twice over, and none on commentary, controls, forecast quality or
scenario — and it is the absence of those, not the absence of the demo, that made me answer no.
